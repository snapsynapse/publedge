General Questions What does the Office of AI Policy do? The Office of Artificial Intelligence Policy (OAIP) is part of the Utah Department of Commerce. The Legislature created our office in May 2024, and we work towards three objectives: Observe and Learn, Protect the Public, and Foster Innovation. In practice that means we study how AI is actually being used in Utah and we recommend changes to state law and rules based on what we learn. We are also authorized to enter agreements that adjust how an existing rule applies to a specific AI product for a limited, supervised period. See Utah Code § 13-72-301 . What authority does the Office of AI Policy have? We have authority to work with the agency that regulates a given industry to craft a regulatory mitigation agreement. We cannot grant one alone. The head of the relevant agency, most often the Utah Division of Professional Licensing, signs alongside us, and no pilot proceeds without that approval. We also cannot rewrite a rule on our own. An agreement adjusts how a rule applies to one participant for a set period; changing the rule itself is for the Legislature or for the agency's own rulemaking process. See Utah Code § 13-72-401 . What does “regulatory mitigation” mean? It means giving a company flexibility in how it satisfies an existing Utah rule, or setting that rule aside for a limited time when doing so serves the public interest. Most of Utah's rules were written before this technology existed, so a rule can block a useful product for reasons that have nothing to do with the harm the rule was written to prevent. The main reason we build these agreements is to gather evidence. A pilot helps us mak data-driven decisions by telling us whether a rule should be updated and what the updated version should say. Is the sandbox exclusively for clinical uses of AI? No. The sandbox is open to any AI application that runs into a state-level Utah rule, in any regulated field. Clinical applications are just a slice of that pie. There are practical reasons for the concentration. Clinical care is among the most heavily regulated activities in the state, and the distance between what these tools can now do and what the existing rules anticipated is wide. If your product sits in a different regulated space, we would still like to hear from you. Eligibility is set in Utah Code § 13-72-402 . What if I want to apply for regulatory mitigation? Email the office at [email protected] . Tell us what your product does, which Utah rule you believe stands in the way, and who would be using it. Before you write, it is worth reading how the application process works . Eligibility criteria are set in Utah Code § 13-72-402 . What are some recent laws OAIP has supported? S.B. 149 (2024) Bill Sponsors: Sen. Kirk Cullimore and Rep. Jefferson Moss. The Artificial Intelligence Policy Act created this office. It established that consumer protection law still applies when a person uses AI, and it requires disclosure when someone is interacting with AI in a regulated occupation. H.B. 452 (2025) Bill Sponsors: Rep. Moss and Sen. Cullimore. The mental health chatbot law set protections for people using an AI mental health chatbot. It limits what such a chatbot may do with personal information and requires it to disclose what it is. By setting clear standards, it also gave companies a defined path to offer these tools in Utah. S.B. 226 (2025) Bill Sponsors: Sen. Cullimore and Rep. Moss. Extended S.B. 149 by requiring disclosure when generative AI is used in a consumer transaction, and confirming liability for consumer protection violations that involve AI. H.B. 276 (2026) Bill Sponsors: Rep. Ariel Defay and Sen. Cullimore. Addresses deepfakes. It gives a person a way to act if someone uses AI to make intimate or private images of them without consent, and it requires digital content provenance, a kind of digital fingerprint that lets a viewer tell whether a photo or video came from a real camera or was generated. H.B. 408 (2026) Bill Sponsors: Rep. Doug Fiefia and Sen. Michael McKell. The Digital Choice Act sets an interoperability standard for social media platforms so a user can download their own data and take it to a competing service. It supports competition by making it easier to leave. What if I have comments or concerns? Please share your feedback with us. We want to hear from you, and what the public tells us shapes what we study next. How We Review and Supervise a Pilot These questions cover who evaluates a proposal before it is approved, and what happens once a pilot is running. Who reviews a clinical AI proposal before it is approved? There are several rounds of review before a pilot is authorized. First, our office reviews pilot proposals. Then we loop in external experts including our Healthcare AI Advisory Network : physicians, pharmacists, nurse practitioners, health services researchers, and health care attorneys who evaluate whether a proposed use is clinically sound. The regulating agency reviews it too, and its approval is required before any pilot proceeds. For the prescribing pilots that is the Division of Professional Licensing. We are still adding to the reviewer roster. If you are a clinician and want to be considered, see Work with Us ; licensure in Utah is not required. Does the company set its own safety benchmarks? No. An applicant can propose benchmarks, but they are negotiated with this office and with the regulating agency, informed by the clinical reviewers, before anything is signed. What ends up in the agreement is what those parties agreed to, and it is published so the standard is not a private one. The statute requires an agreement to specify the limits on scope, the safeguards, the disclosures owed to consumers, and the reporting we will audit against. Those are not optional terms. See Utah Code § 13-72-401(4) . What role do the licensing boards play? A substantial one. The Utah Medical Board has raised concerns with this office about how the prescribing pilots are supervised, and this office has met with the Board to work through them. That exchange is a feature of the arrangement rather than a problem with it: the board that licenses physicians in this state is well placed to tell us where a pilot's oversight is thin. Board meetings are open and their minutes are public. The statute also directs us to consult the relevant agencies on the terms of any agreement. See Utah Code § 13-72-401(5) . What happens if something goes wrong during a pilot? We may terminate an agreement at any time and for any reason. A participant that breaks the law or breaches its agreement can be removed from the program immediately and remains subject to every applicable civil and criminal penalty. Ending a pilot also restores nothing to the participant: the relief simply stops, and the rule applies as it did before. See Utah Code § 13-72-401(8) . How do I report any concerns I have with a pilot? Tell us at [email protected] , and tell us as specifically as you can what happened. First-hand patient experiences inform our audits. Where you report matters, though, and it is worth choosing deliberately. General concerns belong with our office. A complaint about a licensed practitioner's conduct belongs with the Division of Professional Licensing , which holds the license. A deceptive business practice belongs with the Division of Consumer Protection . Where can I see the agreements and the results for myself? The Authorized AI Pilots page lists every pilot with its signed date, its term, what relief was granted, and a link to the agreement itself. The Policy Development page carries our learning agenda and what we have published. We also report to the Legislature each year, before November 30, on the agenda, our findings, the agreements in place, and any legislation we recommend. That report goes to the Business and Labor Interim Committee and is a public document. See Utah Code § 13-72-201 . If something you want is not published, you may request it under the Government Records Access and Management Act through the state records request portal . Does an agreement mean the State of Utah endorses the product? No. Participation in participation in our regulatory sandbox is not an endorsement or approval from the state. The state is also not responsible for claims, liabilities, damages, losses, or expenses arising from a participant's involvement or from what it does under its agreement. What an agreement represents is a decision that a specific use is worth studying under supervision, on stated conditions, for a limited time. See Utah Code § 13-72-401(9) and (10) . Clinical Uses of AI Prescription refills were the first clinical application this office authorized, and the section that follows covers them in detail. The answers here apply to clinical pilots generally, including the applications we expect to see next. Which clinical uses of AI can the sandbox cover? Any clinical activity a Utah rule regulates. Prescription refills came first because the task is narrow and the safety question is easy to state plainly. Participating organizations are listed on the authorized AI pilots page . Will an AI tool diagnose me? Only where an agreement expressly allows it, and only on the terms that agreement sets. No pilot hands a tool an open-ended license to diagnose. Where diagnostic support is authorized, it is bounded on every side: a defined set of conditions, a defined patient population, and a licensed clinician who reviews the work before it changes anyone's care. Pilots begin in the supervised position and move out of it on evidence, not on schedule. Is a licensed clinician still responsible for my care? Yes. Every clinical agreement names licensed professionals who carry responsibility for what the tool does, and the licensing standards that apply to them are untouched. An agreement adjusts how one rule applies to one product for a set period. It does not license AI companies to practice a regulated profession. The rest of Utah law also keeps running during a pilot, including the standards governing clinical practice and the consequences when they are not met. Utah Code § 13-72-401 sets that out. What happens when a case is beyond what the tool is approved to do? It stops and hands the case to a person. Every clinical agreement defines the conditions that force this, and the tool is built so it cannot carry on through one. The specific triggers depend on the application. Across the prescribing pilots they include a sign that a condition is worsening, a mismatch in the patient's identity or record, or a safety concern of any kind. A patient can also ask for a human at any point without giving a reason. Participating organizations must treat escalation as the tool working correctly rather than as a failure. Agreements are written so that a company gains nothing by escalating less. How does the office decide a clinical use is safe enough to test? We start from what could go wrong rather than from what the product promises. A proposal is scored against the proposal rubric for clinical AI , and clinicians outside this office review it through the Healthcare AI Advisory Network . The regulator with authority over the practice area has to agree before anything starts. Approval is then typically staged rather than granted all at once. A tool ordinarily operates under prospective human review first, has to meet a numeric agreement rate with the clinicians checking its work, and only afterward moves to lighter monitoring. Missing a benchmark does not stretch the timeline. It stops the advance. Are some clinical uses off the table? Some are, and the exclusions are written into each agreement rather than left to anyone's judgment in the moment. For example, controlled substances have been excluded from every prescribing pilot so far. Emergency and acute care, patients whose condition is not stable, and higher-risk categories needing continuous monitoring have stayed outside the pilots as well. The rule we apply is straightforward: the greater the harm from a wrong answer, and the harder that harm is to undo, the less willing we are to test it here. Nothing is excluded forever, but the evidence we require rises with the risk. What happens to my health information? Taking part in a pilot loosens no privacy obligation. Health privacy law applies to a participant exactly as it would if this office had never signed anything, because an agreement reaches only the specific rule it names and leaves every other law in force. Utah Code § 13-72-401 states that directly. Participants also report to this office on a schedule set in their agreement. Those reports cover how the tool performed. They are not a channel for identifiable patient records. Do I have to use an AI tool to get care? No. Taking part is the patient's choice, the service has to disclose that AI is involved, and a patient may ask for a licensed clinician at any stage. Nothing in any pilot changes how a Utah resident gets care through their usual provider. Prescription Refills These questions cover the pilots in which an AI tool participates in refilling a prescription a licensed clinician already wrote. How do AI prescriptions work in these pilots? Throughout every one of these pilots, the AI tool may only refill a prescription a licensed physician already ordered. A new prescription, a dose change, or a change in frequency must come from a licensed physician. In the first phase, a licensed physician reviews each refill the AI recommends before it reaches a pharmacist. A company moves to the second phase only after it meets the benchmarks in its agreement and performs well against them. In the second phase, the AI tool may send the refill to the pharmacist directly. Pharmacists keep the authority to escalate any AI-generated refill to a licensed physician at the prescribing company, and each company gives pharmacists a direct way to do it. We design these pilots to change as little as possible about how a pharmacist works. What medications can be prescribed? Each pilot has its own formulary, built with regulators, pharmacists, clinicians, and the company itself. They run from roughly 20 to roughly 200 approved medications, depending on the pilot. These lists cover common medications for chronic conditions and for maintaining mental health. No formulary includes a controlled substance. Every formulary is published in full inside that pilot's signed agreement, which you can read on the Authorized AI Pilots page . If it’s generated by AI, then whose name is on the prescription? A licensed physician's. Every refill is signed and approved by a licensed physician, either directly or through the protocol that physicians set for the AI system. The order is still issued by a licensed practitioner, and that practitioner's name is attached to it. What if a pharmacist needs additional information, clinical clarification, or documentation? Every company in this program must give pharmacists a direct line to a licensed professional it employs. That is a condition of each agreement that includes prescriptions. A patient has to clear identity and prescription verification to qualify for an AI-generated prescription at all, and that process gives the service the original prescribing physician's details, which it can pass to a pharmacist on request. A physician's name is always attached to the authorization. What keeps the system’s refills aligned with what the prescriber intended? Several safeguards, working together. Controlled substances are excluded outright, which keeps these pilots to medications with established safety profiles for routine refill. A patient may also receive only a limited number of refills per medication before an in-person or telehealth visit with a clinician licensed in Utah is required. And the system carries built-in escalation rules. It refers a case to a human physician when the clinical picture is more complex than agreed thresholds allow, when the information it receives conflicts, or when the patient's status suggests a possible medication-related problem. What oversight can the public expect? Every company in the program files a monthly report with this office. Its agreement specifies exactly which metrics that report must carry, and the agreements are published, so anyone can see what a company is required to tell us. We verify the key figures rather than accepting them, and safety is the first among them. Auditing a participant while its agreement is in force is a duty the statute places on this office. See Utah Code § 13-72-401(6) . If an AI tool approves a refill and an adverse event occurs, how is liability determined? The way we structure these pilots is meant to make such an event unlikely, but we do not treat it as impossible. Each company in the program employs a licensed physician whose name appears on the AI-generated refills, so there is an accountable practitioner behind every order. Our agreements also require the company to either carry medical malpractice insurance covering AI-related liability and risk or self-insure. An agreement waives none of a participant's other legal obligations, and a patient's remedies are unaffected by it. See Utah Code § 13-72-401(7) . Is this program permanent? No. These pilots exist under this office's authority to permit an AI use case that needs regulatory relief, for a limited time. An initial demonstration period may not exceed 12 months. A participant may request a 12-month extension, and we may grant up to two. The Doctronic pilot, for example, runs from October 2025 to October 2026, and any continuation past that is a decision this office has to make rather than an automatic renewal. See Utah Code § 13-72-403 . Does this mean any AI can practice medicine in Utah? No. An agreement covers one named company, one described product, and one defined set of tasks, for a limited period, on conditions that company must keep. It grants nothing to anyone else, and it grants nothing at all if the participant stops meeting its terms. We may end an agreement at any time and for any reason, and a participant has no property right or license in taking part. Signing one is also not a state endorsement or approval of the product. See Utah Code § 13-72-401(8) and (9) . What changes have been made to the contract? The Doctronic agreement's phase 2 transition criteria were updated. The pilot previously advanced once 250 prescriptions had been filled in total. Now each medication group advances as a group, once 250 prescriptions have been filled within that group. Moving from phase 1 to phase 2 still requires this office's approval. Butalbital and Flecainide were also removed from Doctronic's formulary.
